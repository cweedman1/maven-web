Incident Investigation Report Example (Nursing Home)

Incident investigations are one of the most important responsibilities in nursing home operations. When an allegation, injury, change in condition, or reportable event occurs, the facility has to respond quickly, protect residents, preserve evidence, and build a record that can be reviewed by leadership, regulators, and surveyors.

The purpose of an investigation is to determine what happened, who was involved, what evidence exists, what actions were taken, and what conclusion is supported by the evidence. The goal is not simply completing paperwork. The goal is conducting a thorough, factual, and defensible investigation.

Investigation Report Structure

A complete nursing home investigation file should make the review path clear: initial report, immediate protective actions, evidence gathered, witness statements, timeline, findings, determination, corrective actions, and follow-up submission. The exact form may vary by state or facility policy, but the file should allow someone reviewing it months later to understand what happened and why the final determination was supported.

Reporting Requirements

Reporting comes first. Certain incidents require immediate reporting, including allegations of abuse, allegations of neglect, misappropriation of property, significant injuries, and crimes involving residents. Some events require reporting within two hours. Other reportable events generally require reporting within twenty-four hours.

Administrators should know their state reporting process, internal notification chain, and after-hours expectations before an event occurs. This page is operational guidance, not legal advice. When reporting obligations are unclear, facilities should prioritize resident safety, document decisions, and escalate concerns promptly.

Immediate Protective Actions

Resident safety comes first. Investigations frequently require immediate protective actions before the investigation is complete. Those actions may include increased monitoring, resident reassessment, environmental correction, staff education, temporary reassignment of staff, or temporary removal of an employee from resident contact when required by facility policy or the nature of the allegation.

Protective actions are not determinations. They are temporary measures used to protect residents while the investigation is conducted. An administrator should be able to explain what was done immediately, why it was done, who was notified, and when the protective action was reviewed.

The Investigation Process

  • Ensure resident safety: Assess the resident, separate involved parties when needed, and arrange medical evaluation or transfer if indicated.
  • Secure evidence: Preserve relevant records, video, photographs, equipment, assignments, logs, and environmental observations.
  • Notify appropriate parties: Notify leadership, physician or practitioner, resident representative, state agency, law enforcement, or others as required by the event.
  • Gather statements: Obtain timely statements from residents, staff, witnesses, agency personnel, visitors, and others with direct knowledge.
  • Review documentation: Review progress notes, care plans, MAR/TAR records, incident notes, assessments, staffing sheets, and prior related concerns.
  • Build the timeline: Put events in order so the reviewer can see exactly what occurred and when.
  • Analyze findings: Compare statements, records, observations, and physical evidence.
  • Determine outcome: Decide whether the allegation or event is substantiated, unsubstantiated, unfounded, or inconclusive based on the evidence.
  • Implement corrective action: Address immediate risk and underlying process issues.
  • Submit final investigation: Complete the follow-up submission with findings, determination, corrective action, and supporting documentation.

Building the Timeline

The timeline is often the most important component of the investigation. It should document what happened, when it happened, who was involved, notifications made, actions taken, medical response, observations, and hospital transfer information when applicable.

A strong timeline should allow someone reviewing the investigation months later to understand exactly what occurred. Weak timelines weaken otherwise good investigations. Avoid vague entries like "staff notified" or "resident assessed" without time, person, action, and observation details.

Writing the Narrative

The narrative should tell a factual chronological story. It should align with witness statements, resident interviews, staff interviews, medical records, observations, and notifications. The narrative is not the place to argue, speculate, or fill in gaps.

The strongest investigation narratives are supported by evidence. If statements conflict, document the conflict and explain what the investigation ultimately found. If the chart, interview, and observation do not line up, say so clearly. Avoid assumptions, speculation, and unsupported conclusions. The narrative should stand on its own months later.

Determinations

The investigator is responsible for making a determination. Common outcomes include substantiated, unsubstantiated, unfounded, and inconclusive.

  • Substantiated: the evidence supports that the allegation or event occurred.
  • Unsubstantiated: the available evidence does not support the allegation or event as reported.
  • Unfounded: the investigation supports that the allegation or event did not occur.
  • Inconclusive: reasonable investigative efforts were completed, but there is not enough evidence to support or refute the allegation.

The determination should be supported by evidence, statements, observations, documentation, and timeline analysis. The goal is not defending a conclusion. The goal is following the evidence wherever it leads. A determination that is not supported by the investigation record creates risk during regulatory follow-up.

Root Cause Analysis

Investigations should identify why an event occurred, not simply document that it occurred. The Five Whys method can help the administrator push past the first answer. A fishbone analysis can help organize contributing factors such as staffing, communication, environment, equipment, documentation, supervision, or workflow.

The goal is identifying process failures, communication failures, monitoring failures, workflow failures, and accountability gaps. Many investigations ultimately become QAPI opportunities. If the same type of event keeps happening, the facility likely has a process problem, not just an isolated incident.

Corrective Actions

Investigations should result in action. Corrective actions may include staff education, policy revision, process changes, environmental changes, monitoring programs, competency validation, supervision changes, or QAPI referral.

Corrective actions should address the cause, not just the event. If the issue was a communication failure between shifts, a one-time staff in-service may not be enough. If the issue was missed monitoring, the corrective action should include a practical monitoring process and someone responsible for reviewing it.

Follow-Up Submission

Most reportable investigations require a complete follow-up submission after the initial report. The final submission should include findings, determination, corrective actions, supporting documentation, and monitoring plans.

Accuracy, consistency, and completeness matter. The final report should align with statements, timeline, medical record, notifications, and corrective actions. If the investigation file contradicts itself, surveyors will usually find it.

Common Investigation Mistakes

  • Reaching conclusions before facts are gathered
  • Weak or incomplete timelines
  • Missing resident, staff, or witness statements
  • Unsupported determinations
  • Incomplete documentation or missing attachments
  • Failure to connect corrective actions to findings
